Digital Resistance, Disinformation, and the Rule of Law: Navigating the Hybrid Conflict Between Russia and the West
The Digital Maidan: Ukraine's Mobilization of Blockchain Technology
In the crucible of the 2022 full-scale invasion, Ukraine demonstrated a remarkable capacity for technological adaptation, transforming blockchain and cryptocurrency from a niche interest into a potent instrument of national defense and statecraft. Building on a pre-existing high level of digital currency adoption among its populace, the Ukrainian government swiftly mobilized the global crypto community, pioneering a new paradigm in conflict financing and governance. This rapid, state-led embrace of decentralized technology not only provided a critical financial lifeline in the war's desperate early days but also offered innovative tools for countering disinformation and laying the groundwork for post-war reconstruction. Ukraine’s experience provides a compelling case study in how emerging technologies can be leveraged as strategic assets in modern hybrid warfare, fundamentally altering the dynamics of national resilience and international support.
Crypto-Fundraising as an Instrument of War: A New Paradigm in Conflict Financing
Within days of the Russian invasion on February 24, 2022, the Ukrainian government initiated an unprecedented global fundraising campaign, turning directly to the world's cryptocurrency users for support. This move was a strategic masterstroke, leveraging the borderless and rapid nature of digital assets to circumvent the slower, more bureaucratic channels of traditional finance. In the initial, chaotic phase of the conflict, Ukraine received approximately $100 million in cryptocurrency donations, a figure that grew to over $212 million for various pro-Ukrainian efforts within the first year of the war.
The most critical advantage of this approach was its velocity. At a time when conventional wire transfers or humanitarian aid could take one to three days to clear banking systems, cryptocurrency donations reached their destination in seconds. This speed was not a mere convenience; it was a decisive tactical factor. Michael Chobanian, President of the Blockchain Association of Ukraine, explained to the U.S. Congress that these funds could be received and almost instantly used to purchase vital supplies like helmets and ammunition or to send financial support to besieged cities. This immediate liquidity provided the Ukrainian defense effort with a level of agility that traditional financing mechanisms could not match.
Central to this effort was the "Aid For Ukraine" initiative, an official fundraising platform established through a partnership between Ukraine's Ministry of Digital Transformation, the Ukraine-based cryptocurrency exchange Kuna, and the staking platform Everstake. This platform served as a legitimate, government-backed channel for donations, building trust within the often-skeptical crypto community. It demonstrated Ukraine's technological sophistication by accepting a diverse portfolio of digital assets, including Bitcoin (BTC), Ethereum (ETH), Tether (USDT), Solana (SOL), and Polkadot (DOT), thereby maximizing its potential donor base.
The campaign's innovation extended beyond simply posting wallet addresses. It employed fundraising tactics native to the crypto world to galvanize support. A notable example was the use of Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs). Ukraine DAO, a decentralized autonomous organization formed to support the country, auctioned an NFT of the Ukrainian flag that raised $6.75 million. Furthermore, the Ukrainian government announced an "airdrop"—a crypto-native term for the distribution of new tokens to existing wallet holders—as a reward for those who donated within a specific timeframe. Research conducted by Professor Jane Tan at SMU Cox revealed the dramatic impact of this incentive: donation counts for Ethereum, the primary blockchain for the airdrop, increased by over 700% more than for Bitcoin following the announcement. This highlighted a sophisticated understanding of the crypto community's motivations, where the potential for investment value in a reward can be a powerful driver of philanthropic behavior.
From Donations to Defense: Translating Digital Assets into Battlefield Materiel
The hundreds of millions of dollars raised were not abstract figures on a digital ledger; they were rapidly converted into tangible, life-saving assets for the Armed Forces of Ukraine and for humanitarian programs. The "Aid For Ukraine" initiative has maintained a degree of public transparency regarding its expenditures, providing detailed reports that lend credibility to the effort and counter skepticism about the real-world utility of crypto donations. This level of reporting is a crucial element, demonstrating accountability and showing donors the direct impact of their contributions.
The procurement list funded by these donations is extensive and illustrates the comprehensive nature of the support. It covers both lethal and non-lethal military equipment, as well as critical humanitarian supplies. The war has served as a powerful catalyst, compelling the Ukrainian state to leverage its existing grassroots expertise in digital currency and formalize it into a strategic national asset. The urgent need for fast, borderless funding created the political will to overcome previous legislative hurdles, leading to a virtuous cycle: the success of the crypto-fundraising campaign demonstrated the technology's power, which in turn justified and accelerated the passage of a legal framework designed to support a digitized post-war economy. This represents a rare instance of a nation constructing a sophisticated digital economic architecture not in peacetime, but in the midst of a full-scale defensive war.
The public expenditure reports from "Aid For Ukraine" and its partners provide a granular look at how digital assets were deployed on the ground. The funds were used to acquire a wide array of military hardware, including 357 Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) at a cost of nearly $15.6 million, 8,558 armor vests, 4,317 helmets, and 5,082 digital rifle scopes. Significant funds were also allocated to items for which details remain non-public for security reasons, such as $5.1 million for "lethal equipment" and over $5.7 million for "specialized hardware and software complexes".
Beyond direct armaments, the donations funded the logistical and support backbone of the military effort. This included over $3.8 million for 416,900 field rations, nearly $1.2 million for 31,065 military medical kits, and over $2.1 million for 165 vehicles. The funds were even used for information warfare, with over $8.3 million dedicated to a "worldwide anti-war media campaign," the details of which are to be published after the war. 	Beyond Fundraising: Blockchain's Role in Governance and Resilience
While the fundraising campaign captured global headlines, Ukraine's use of blockchain technology extends far beyond financial transactions into the realms of governance, evidence preservation, and humanitarian aid. These applications demonstrate a deeper, more strategic integration of the technology's core attributes—immutability, transparency, and decentralization—to enhance state functions and build societal resilience against a multifaceted Russian assault.
A critical application has been in countering Russia's pervasive disinformation campaigns and documenting war crimes. The Russian strategy relies heavily on creating a "firehose of falsehood" to obscure facts, deny atrocities like the Bucha massacre, and sow confusion. In a direct asymmetric response, Ukraine has weaponized transparency. The government supported the development of an app called "E-Enemy," which empowers ordinary citizens to use their smartphones to photograph, video, and geolocate Russian attacks and potential war crimes. This crowdsourced data provides a powerful, first-person evidentiary record. To ensure its integrity, this evidence can be cryptographically "hashed"—a process that creates a unique, tamper-proof digital fingerprint—and recorded on a blockchain. This creates an immutable, decentralized dossier of atrocities that is resilient to Russian hacking or propaganda efforts and can be used for future prosecutions by international bodies like the International Criminal Court (ICC). This is not merely data collection; it is the creation of an objective historical record designed to pre-emptively arm the international legal system with credible evidence.
The potential of blockchain to improve the efficiency and accountability of governance is also being explored. The United Nations Refugee Agency (UNHCR) successfully piloted a project in Ukraine that used a blockchain-based system to deliver financial assistance directly to internally displaced people. This model allows aid to be transferred securely and transparently, ensuring it reaches its intended recipients who can then convert it to cash for essential needs like food, rent, and medicine. This approach mitigates the risks of corruption and mismanagement that can plague traditional foreign aid distribution, showcasing a path toward more effective humanitarian response.
These wartime innovations are built upon a pre-existing foundation. Even before the 2022 invasion, Ukraine was experimenting with blockchain for governance. In 2017, the government agreed to implement blockchain technology to help manage the State Register of Property Rights and a system for the electronic trading of seized property. These early pilot projects in areas like land auctions were designed to increase transparency and combat corruption, a persistent challenge for the country. This prior experience and institutional knowledge provided a crucial springboard for the rapid deployment of blockchain solutions once the full-scale war began, demonstrating a long-term vision for digital transformation that the conflict has only accelerated.
The Legal Architecture: Formalizing the Digital Economy
Ukraine's embrace of cryptocurrency during the war was not an ad-hoc, unregulated free-for-all. It was underpinned by a deliberate and swift move to create a formal legal framework, signaling a long-term strategic commitment to integrating digital assets into its economy. On March 16, 2022, just weeks into the invasion, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy signed the "On Virtual Assets" bill into law. This act was a pivotal moment, transforming Ukraine into one of the first countries to establish a comprehensive legal regime for cryptocurrencies while simultaneously fighting a major war.
The law provided much-needed regulatory clarity. It established the legal status, classification, and ownership rights for virtual assets, and it allowed both Ukrainian and foreign cryptocurrency exchanges to operate legally within the country. Critically, the framework mandated clear rules for Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Know Your Customer (KYC) compliance, aligning Ukraine's digital asset market with international financial standards. The law also streamlined oversight by designating a single regulatory body, the National Commission on Securities and Stock Market, to supervise the industry. This resolved a key issue that had led President Zelenskyy to veto a previous version of the bill, which would have created a costly new supervisory agency.
The timing of the law's passage is deeply significant. It demonstrates that the Ukrainian government views digital assets not merely as a temporary fundraising tool but as a foundational component of its future economic strategy. Ukrainian officials have been explicit about this vision. Alex Bornyakov, the Deputy Minister of Digital Transformation, has stated that crypto is playing a "significant role in Ukraine's defense". Mike Chobanian of the Blockchain Association of Ukraine went further, framing the conflict as a "war against centralized regime" and heralding the "birth of blockchain as a backbone of global security". He articulated a clear post-war objective, stating that virtual assets "will be the technology that we can use to rebuild our country". This forward-looking perspective suggests that the wartime innovations in digital finance and governance are intended to be permanent, forming the basis of a more transparent, efficient, and digitized post-war economy that is attractive to foreign investment and resilient to corruption.
The Ecosystem of Deception: Russia's Information Warfare Apparatus
The Russian Federation wages a relentless and sophisticated information war against the West, employing a complex, multi-layered ecosystem of state and proxy actors to disseminate disinformation. This apparatus is not a monolithic propaganda machine but a dynamic network designed to erode trust in democratic institutions, fracture societal cohesion, and undermine the U.S.-led international order. Its primary method is the "firehose of falsehood," a high-volume, multi-channel strategy that prioritizes overwhelming audiences over narrative consistency. Understanding the structure, tactics, and strategic goals of this ecosystem is essential for comprehending the nature of modern hybrid conflict and for crafting effective countermeasures.
The "Firehose of Falsehood": Pillars of the Disinformation Ecosystem
The operational doctrine of Russia's information warfare has been described as a "firehose of falsehood" due to its high number of channels and its unabashed willingness to disseminate propaganda that is not only false but often internally contradictory. This approach differs markedly from traditional propaganda, which typically seeks to build a coherent and believable alternative narrative. Instead, the Russian model aims to create an overwhelming volume of noise, making it difficult for audiences to distinguish fact from fiction and ultimately fostering cynicism and distrust toward all sources of information, including credible media and government institutions.
The U.S. State Department has identified a five-pillar structure that constitutes Russia's disinformation and propaganda ecosystem. These pillars work in concert, reinforcing and amplifying each other to maximize reach and impact. The five pillars are:
1. Official Government Communications: Direct statements, press briefings, and social media posts from Kremlin officials, ministries (such as the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Defense), and embassies. These provide the top-level messaging that is then echoed throughout the ecosystem.
2. State-Funded Global Messaging: A global network of multilingual media outlets, most prominently RT (formerly Russia Today) and Sputnik. These platforms present themselves as alternative news sources to Western audiences but are under the direct control of the Russian government and serve as its primary conduits for international propaganda.
3. Cultivation of Proxy Sources: A network of websites, think tanks, and online journals that masquerade as independent news or research outlets but are in fact directed and controlled by Russian intelligence services. These proxies launder Kremlin narratives, giving them a veneer of authenticity and academic credibility.
4. Weaponization of Social Media: The use of social media platforms to create and amplify disinformation. This is executed through vast networks of bots, trolls, and fake personas, often managed by entities like the Internet Research Agency (IRA), to manipulate public discourse, inflame social divisions, and spread divisive content.
5. Cyber-Enabled Disinformation: The use of hacking and other malicious cyber activities to support information operations. This can involve stealing and selectively leaking sensitive information (hack-and-leak operations), defacing websites, or conducting distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks to silence dissenting voices.
This ecosystem approach provides Russia with significant tactical advantages. The sheer number of outlets allows for the simultaneous dissemination of multiple, even conflicting, versions of a story, a tactic designed to confuse and exhaust audiences. Furthermore, the use of proxies provides plausible deniability; the Kremlin can distance itself from the most blatant falsehoods while still benefiting from their circulation in the information environment.
Anatomy of the Disinformation Actors: A Network of State and Proxy Entities
The operational command and control of Russia's information warfare apparatus reside within its state intelligence services, which direct a sprawling network of media outlets, proxy organizations, and cyber actors. This structure combines the resources of the state with the deniability of non-state actors, creating a formidable and resilient system.
At the apex are Russia's primary intelligence agencies. The Main Intelligence Directorate of the General Staff (GRU) is heavily involved in cyber-enabled disinformation. Its specialized units, such as APT28 (also known as Fancy Bear or STRONTIUM) and Unit 74455 (also known as Sandworm), are responsible for some of the most aggressive cyber operations, including hacking political organizations, conducting destructive malware attacks, and carrying out sophisticated credential-harvesting campaigns. The Federal Security Service (FSB), the successor to the KGB, also conducts its own malicious cyber operations and has been known to task criminal hacker groups to carry out espionage-focused activities on its behalf.
A key operational component is the Internet Research Agency (IRA), the infamous St. Petersburg-based "troll farm" financed by oligarch Yevgeniy Prigozhin. The IRA is a factory for inauthentic behavior, creating thousands of fake online personas, automated bot accounts, and entire counterfeit community groups across platforms like Facebook, Twitter, and Reddit. Its tactics have evolved beyond simple trolling; it has co-opted unwitting activists in foreign countries, such as Ghana, to more effectively target specific demographics in the United States, particularly Black communities. The IRA's purpose is to inject divisive narratives into online conversations, amplify social and racial conflicts, and interfere directly in the democratic processes of target nations.
The most visible pillar is the state-funded media, led by RT and Sputnik. These outlets operate globally in multiple languages, tailoring their content to specific audiences. They often mimic the style and tone of populist or alternative media within a target country, a tactic designed to build credibility and attract an audience that is already skeptical of mainstream sources. They provide a platform for local influencers, academics, and politicians whose views align with Kremlin interests, thereby laundering Russian propaganda through seemingly authentic, domestic voices.
Perhaps the most sophisticated layer of the ecosystem is the network of proxy websites and organizations that deliberately obscure their connections to the Russian state. These entities function as "gray propaganda" outlets, lending an air of independence or academic rigor to Kremlin-backed narratives. Countermeasures targeting one pillar, such as sanctioning state media, do not cripple the entire operation; they merely force a shift in emphasis to other parts of the network, which continue to propagate the core messages through multiple, seemingly independent channels.
Strategic Imperatives: The Goal of Dismantling Western Cohesion
The tactical operations of Russia's disinformation ecosystem serve a clear and consistent set of strategic imperatives aimed at weakening its adversaries from within and reshaping the global geopolitical landscape in its favor. These goals range from the immediate disruption of democratic processes to the long-term erosion of the entire Western-led international order.
The most immediate and frequently observed goal is the undermining of democratic institutions and processes. The primary objective, as identified in U.S. intelligence assessments, is to "sow discord in the U.S. political system" and in other Western democracies. This is not achieved by inventing new divisions, but by skillfully exploiting and amplifying pre-existing societal fissures. Russian information operations target contentious issues such as race, immigration, gun control, and public health, injecting inflammatory content to fuel polarization and erode social trust. By flooding the information space with a mixture of real and fabricated information, these campaigns make it difficult for citizens to discern the truth, leading to a decline in trust in core democratic institutions, including elections, the media, and government itself.
A second, parallel strategic imperative is the weakening of transatlantic and European alliances. A significant portion of Russian disinformation is aimed at undermining public faith in cornerstone Western alliances like the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) and the European Union (EU). Narratives are crafted to portray NATO as an aggressive, expansionist entity, to question the value of collective defense commitments, and to foster distrust among member states. In the context of the war in Ukraine, these campaigns seek to undermine international support for Kyiv by spreading falsehoods that portray Ukraine as a corrupt and failing state, question the effectiveness of Western aid, and suggest that sanctions are harming Western economies more than Russia's. The goal is to fracture the political consensus that underpins the Western response to Russian aggression.
At the highest strategic level, Russia's information warfare is an assault on the liberal international order itself. The Kremlin seeks to dismantle the post-Cold War, U.S.-led global system and replace it with a "multipolar world" in which Russia reclaims its status as a great power with a recognized sphere of influence. To this end, its propaganda promotes a set of alternative ideological narratives. These include the concept of the "Russian World" (Russkiy Mir) to justify influence over Russian-speaking populations, the notion of "Slavic Unity" to build support for its geopolitical agenda, and nostalgia for the Soviet era to position Russia as a conservative, traditionalist counterbalance to a supposedly "decadent," "satanic," and hegemonic West. This is a direct ideological challenge to the principles of liberal democracy, national sovereignty, and universal human rights that form the foundation of the Western order.
A key tactic in achieving these goals is the "outsourcing" of disinformation to Western voices. Rather than relying solely on Russian officials or journalists, who may lack credibility with foreign audiences, entities like New Eastern Outlook (NEO) and the Strategic Culture Foundation (SCF) actively recruit and provide a platform for Western fringe thinkers, conspiracy theorists, and academics who are critical of their own governments. This is a sophisticated psychological operation that leverages the principle of in-group preference: a message is often more persuasive when it comes from a source perceived to be part of one's own national or political group. By amplifying these voices, Russia launders its propaganda, making it appear as organic, homegrown dissent rather than foreign interference, thus allowing it to penetrate target societies more effectively.
The Sanctions Counteroffensive: Targeting Russia's Propaganda Machine
In response to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine and its persistent information warfare, the United States, the European Union, and the United Kingdom have deployed a range of economic sanctions aimed at crippling the Kremlin's propaganda machine. This counteroffensive has targeted the financial lifelines, broadcasting capabilities, and key personnel of Russia's disinformation ecosystem. While these measures have had a tangible impact, their effectiveness has been limited by Russia's adaptive evasion tactics, inconsistent enforcement across jurisdictions, and the complex legal and ethical dilemma of balancing national security with the fundamental right to freedom of expression.
A Multi-Front Economic Assault: The Sanctions Regimes
The Western sanctions response has been multi-pronged, with the U.S., EU, and UK employing distinct but often overlapping legal instruments to target Russia's disinformation actors.
The United States has primarily used its authority to designate individuals and entities involved in malicious cyber activities and election interference. Through various Executive Orders, the U.S. Treasury Department has added key players in the Russian disinformation network to its Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) List. This designation effectively blocks their assets under U.S. jurisdiction and prohibits U.S. persons from engaging in any transactions with them. Prominent targets have included the Internet Research Agency (IRA), its financier Yevgeniy Prigozhin, and numerous IRA operatives directly involved in the 2016 election interference campaign. The U.S. has also sanctioned Russian-backed media companies as part of broader sanctions packages, complementing financial restrictions with export controls on technology that could support their operations.
The European Union has taken the most direct and sweeping measures against the media outlets themselves. In a series of sanctions packages since March 2022, the EU has suspended the broadcasting activities of more than 30 Russian state-controlled media organizations, including RT, Sputnik, RIA Novosti, Izvestia, and Rossiyskaya Gazeta. This comprehensive ban applies to all means of transmission and distribution within the EU, including cable, satellite, internet platforms, websites, and mobile applications. Alongside the broadcasting ban, the EU has imposed asset freezes and travel bans on dozens of individual Russian propagandists, including prominent journalists, media managers, and government officials responsible for directing the information war.
The United Kingdom, operating its own post-Brexit sanctions regime under the Sanctions and Anti-Money Laundering Act 2018, has mirrored many of the EU and U.S. actions by imposing asset freezes and travel bans on key individuals and entities. In addition, the UK is pursuing a legislative approach through its landmark Online Safety Bill. A proposed amendment to the bill would add foreign state-sponsored disinformation to a list of "priority offences," which would legally compel large technology platforms to proactively address such content on their services. This represents a move toward embedding counter-disinformation obligations directly into the regulatory framework for digital platforms.
Effectiveness, Enforcement, and Evasion: A Leaky Containment
The sanctions have undeniably disrupted Russia's information operations and increased their cost and complexity. The EU's broadcasting ban, in particular, had an immediate and measurable impact. Independent analysis showed that in the three months following the March 2022 ban, aggregate traffic on the domains of sanctioned Russian media outlets within the EU dropped by 74% compared to the previous year. Member states perceive the restrictions as a valuable tool for officially attributing disinformation campaigns to their perpetrators and limiting the operational capacity of Russian-linked media on EU territory.
However, the long-term effectiveness of this containment strategy is significantly undermined by Russia's strategic adaptation and persistent enforcement challenges. The Kremlin's disinformation apparatus has proven to be resilient and agile, employing a variety of tactics to circumvent the sanctions. These include:
* Mirror Websites: A common tactic is the creation of "mirror" websites that host identical content to the banned outlets but operate under slightly different domain names, making them harder for regulators to track and block.
* Platform Migration: Disinformation actors have shifted their focus to less-regulated or encrypted platforms, such as Telegram, which has become a primary channel for coordinating campaigns and disseminating false narratives beyond the reach of Western content moderation.
* Use of VPNs: The widespread availability of Virtual Private Networks (VPNs) allows motivated users to easily bypass geographical restrictions, rendering website blocks ineffective for a segment of the audience.
These evasion tactics are compounded by significant gaps in enforcement. The implementation of sanctions, particularly the online broadcasting ban, is inconsistent across EU member states. Few countries have established robust national mechanisms for systematically monitoring the internet, identifying circumvention efforts, and removing or blocking content that duplicates banned material. This lack of a harmonized enforcement approach creates exploitable loopholes. For instance, while Lithuania has been proactive in blocking IP addresses, other member states have been slower to act, and differing interpretations of financial restrictions mean some sanctioned services remain available in parts of the EU.
In response to being partially contained in the West, Russia has executed a strategic pivot. It has adapted its methods, moving away from overt broadcasting toward more targeted, social media-native campaigns that are harder to attribute. Concurrently, it has intensified its information operations in regions where its state media is not banned and its anti-Western narratives often find a more receptive audience, such as Latin America, Africa, and the Middle East. This demonstrates that sanctions, while disruptive, have acted as an evolutionary pressure, forcing Russia's disinformation tactics to become more covert, resilient, and geographically dispersed.
The Freedom of Speech Dilemma: Legal and Ethical Debates
The Western sanctions regime against Russian disinformation operates within a sensitive legal and ethical landscape, forcing democratic societies to confront the inherent tension between protecting national security and upholding the fundamental right to freedom of expression. This dilemma has been a focal point of legal challenges and political debate.
The most significant legal test came when RT France challenged the EU's broadcasting ban before the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), arguing that the measure was an unlawful infringement on the freedom of expression. In a landmark ruling, the CJEU upheld the sanctions. The Court's reasoning provided a crucial legal framework for distinguishing between protected speech and hostile state propaganda. It affirmed that an interference with freedom of expression can be lawful if it meets four conditions: it must have a legal basis, respect the essence of the right, serve a legitimate objective of general interest, and be a proportional limitation.
The CJEU concluded that the EU's ban met these criteria. It found that the listed media outlets are "under the permanent direct or indirect control of the leadership of the Russian Federation" and are used to "channel propaganda action" that constitutes a "significant and direct threat to the Union's public order and security". The Court deemed the ban proportional, noting that it was targeted specifically at broadcasting activities and did not prevent the outlets from conducting other work, such as research or interviews, within the EU. This judgment is pivotal because it establishes that the source and intent of speech—in this case, a state actor conducting a hostile influence operation to support a war of aggression—are relevant legal considerations, not just the content itself.
This ruling provides a legal bulwark against Russia's strategic use of democratic principles as a weapon against democracies. A core Russian tactic is to frame any countermeasures as an attack on free speech, a form of political and legal jiu-jitsu designed to paralyze a democratic response by co-opting a core Western value. The CJEU's decision helps to legally differentiate between the marketplace of ideas and a state-sponsored information assault. This same debate is playing out in the UK with the Online Safety Bill, which grapples with how to regulate "legal but harmful" content without creating a "censor's charter," and in the U.S., where counter-disinformation efforts have been curtailed due to political accusations of domestic censorship.

	The Rule of Law in the Information War: International Courts and Legal Principles
As Russia's information warfare has become an integral component of its military and political strategy, international legal bodies and principles are increasingly being mobilized to hold the Kremlin accountable. A significant evolution is underway, reframing state-sponsored disinformation not as protected speech but as a prohibited state action with severe legal consequences. Cases before the International Court of Justice (ICJ) and the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR), along with legal analyses of Russia's interference in democratic processes, are building a body of precedent that treats disinformation as a violation of state sovereignty, the principle of non-intervention, and fundamental human rights.
The International Court of Justice (ICJ): Setting Precedents on Disinformation as a Pretext for War
The International Court of Justice, the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, has become a central arena for challenging the legality of Russia's actions, including the disinformation narratives used to justify them.
The case of Ukraine v. Russian Federation, brought under the Genocide Convention, is a landmark in the legal response to information warfare. Ukraine’s core argument was not that Russia had committed genocide, but that Russia had wrongfully and falsely claimed a genocide was occurring in the Donbas region to create a fraudulent legal pretext for its 2022 invasion. This brilliantly turned Russia's primary disinformation narrative against it. On March 16, 2022, the ICJ issued a powerful ruling, ordering provisional measures that required the Russian Federation to "immediately suspend the military operations". In its reasoning, the Court established that states cannot unilaterally use military force on another state's territory to prevent or punish a purported genocide, effectively ruling that Russia's disinformation-based justification was invalid under international law. This decision created a direct legal link between a state's disinformation campaign and its violation of the prohibition on the unlawful use of force.
A second case, Ukraine v. Russian Federation (Application of the International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism and of the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination), further illustrates the legal battlefield. In its judgment of January 31, 2024, the Court found that Russia had violated some of its obligations under the treaties, such as failing to investigate terrorism financing, but did not uphold all of Ukraine's claims. Pro-Kremlin media outlets immediately seized upon the nuanced and complex ruling, misrepresenting it as a wholesale rejection of Ukraine's case and falsely claiming that the "ICJ refused to recognise Russia as an aggressor state". This is a textbook example of Russia's meta-strategy: using the very proceedings of international law as raw material for further disinformation, manipulating legal complexities to craft simple, misleading narratives for both domestic and international consumption.
The European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR): Defending Expression Against State Suppression
While the ICJ addresses state-to-state disputes, the European Court of Human Rights has focused on the internal dimension of Russia's information war: the systematic suppression of truth and dissent within its own borders. In a significant ruling on February 11, 2025, the ECtHR found that the Russian Federation had violated Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which guarantees the right to freedom of expression.
The case consolidated 161 individual applications from Russian citizens, journalists, and media outlets who had been prosecuted for their anti-war activism and reporting. The Court condemned Russia's passage of legislation prohibiting the dissemination of "undesirable information" about the war, which led to the blocking of independent news outlets like Novaya Gazeta and Dozhd TV, as well as international services like the BBC and Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty. It found that the fines, arrests, and prosecutions of individuals for actions as simple as holding a "No to War" sign were disproportionate and unnecessary in a democratic society. This ruling underscores a critical symbiotic relationship: the Kremlin's ability to project a "firehose of falsehood" abroad is contingent upon its creation of an information vacuum at home. By silencing all independent media and criminalizing dissent, the state creates a captive audience for its propaganda and prevents its citizens from accessing alternative narratives that would expose the official lies. The internal repression is the essential logistical and psychological foundation for the external information war.
Disinformation as a Breach of Fundamental International Law Principles
Beyond specific treaty violations, Russia's information operations are increasingly being analyzed as breaches of the most fundamental principles of international law: state sovereignty, non-intervention, and the right to self-determination.
The Russian interference campaign during the 2024 Romanian presidential elections provides a stark case study. A massive, coordinated disinformation campaign, primarily on TikTok, propelled a fringe, pro-Russian candidate with anti-EU and anti-NATO views from obscurity to a surprise first-round victory. The interference was so blatant—involving over 25,000 TikTok accounts, 5,000 Telegram channels, and the preferential treatment of the candidate by the platform's algorithms—that Romania's Constitutional Court took the unprecedented step of annulling the election results, citing "Russian hybrid actions".
Legal analysis of this event concludes that it represents a clear violation of international law.
* Violation of Sovereignty and Non-Intervention: The principle of non-intervention prohibits a state from coercively interfering in the domaine réservé (internal affairs) of another state, which unequivocally includes the holding of free and fair elections. The Russian campaign was not merely an act of expression; its scale, coordination, and covert nature constituted a coercive act intended to manipulate the outcome of the vote and alter Romania's political orientation. This directly infringed upon Romania's supreme authority over its own territory and political processes.
* Violation of the Right to Self-Determination: The campaign also violated the right of the Romanian people to self-determination. As applied to "peoples organised as States," this right prohibits external meddling that seriously infringes upon a people's ability to "freely to determine [its] political status and economic, social and cultural development". The victim of the disinformation was not just the Romanian state as an abstract entity, but the Romanian people, whose collective will was deliberately distorted by a foreign power.
This analysis signals a crucial shift in international law, moving from a purely "content-based" view of speech to an "actor-based" and "effects-based" assessment of information operations. The focus is less on the simple falsity of the information and more on the identity of the disseminator (a state actor), their coercive intent (to subvert a democratic process or justify an illegal war), and the ultimate effect (a violation of another state's sovereignty).
This evolving legal consensus is further supported by older but relevant treaties. Legal scholars have argued that Russia's war propaganda and "genocide disinformation" are clear breaches of the 1936 International Convention Concerning the Use of Broadcasting in the Cause of Peace. This treaty obligates state parties to prohibit any transmissions that incite war or "harm good international understanding by statements the incorrectness of which is or ought to be known". Although Russia entered a reservation to the treaty's jurisdictional clause that prevents an automatic ICJ case without its consent, the convention's substantive provisions still establish the underlying conduct as unlawful under international law.

	A Comparative Analysis of Western Counter-Disinformation Strategies
Western democracies have adopted varied and evolving strategies to counter Russian disinformation, reflecting their distinct political cultures, legal frameworks, and institutional structures. A comparative analysis of the approaches taken by the United States, the United Kingdom, and the multilateral bodies of the European Union and NATO reveals a spectrum of responses, from fragmented and politically constrained efforts to more coordinated and regulatory-focused models. These differences highlight a fundamental debate over the most effective way to build resilience against information warfare in open societies.
The United States: A Fragmented and Politicized Approach
The United States' strategy for countering foreign disinformation has been characterized by a distributed, agency-led approach that has become increasingly fragmented and hobbled by domestic political polarization. Key efforts were historically spread across several bodies. The State Department's Global Engagement Center (GEC) was tasked with leading federal efforts to counter foreign propaganda. The FBI's Foreign Influence Task Force (FITF) was created in 2017 to coordinate investigations into covert foreign influence operations. The Department of Homeland Security's Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) established a Mis-, Dis-, and Malinformation (MDM) team and a "Rumor Control" website to pre-bunk election-related disinformation.
However, these initiatives have faced severe headwinds. Intense political polarization has led to accusations that government efforts to counter foreign disinformation are a pretext for censoring domestic conservative speech. This narrative gained traction and resulted in significant institutional setbacks. CISA suspended its election-related disinformation monitoring, the Department of Homeland Security's short-lived Disinformation Governance Board was disbanded after a public outcry, and the FBI's FITF was dismantled. This effective "unilateral disarming" in the information space has created a dangerous vacuum in the U.S. national security posture, reflecting a debilitating false choice between defending freedom of speech and defending the nation against foreign manipulation.
The efficacy of Western counter-disinformation efforts appears to be inversely correlated with the level of internal political polarization. The U.S. response is a prime example of how domestic political battles can paralyze a coherent national security strategy. In contrast, the EU and NATO, while facing their own internal disagreements, have managed to achieve a broader political consensus on the nature of the threat from Russia, enabling them to mount a more cohesive and robust multilateral defense. This suggests that the primary vulnerability exploited by Russian disinformation is not technological but socio-political. Russia's most effective tactic is to turn a democracy's greatest strength—open and vigorous debate—into a crippling weakness.
In response to this fragmentation, policy bodies like the Cyberspace Solarium Commission have advocated for a new approach. Their recommendations emphasize a "whole-of-society" defense that shifts focus toward building resilience from the bottom up. This includes funding non-governmental researchers, promoting digital literacy and civic education, empowering state and local governments to serve as trusted sources of information, and formally integrating "information integrity" into the National Cyber Strategy to better mobilize public-private partnerships.
The United Kingdom: A Centralized but Evolving Framework
The United Kingdom's approach is more centralized than that of the U.S. but is similarly navigating the complex terrain between regulation and free expression. The cornerstone of its operational response is the Counter Disinformation Unit (CDU), which sits within the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology. The CDU's mandate is to use publicly available data to understand disinformation narratives that pose a risk to public health, safety, or national security, with a current focus on Russian disinformation related to the war in Ukraine. It does not monitor individuals and its primary role is not content removal. Instead, it works with social media platforms to encourage the promotion of authoritative information (e.g., from the National Health Service) and the consistent enforcement of their own terms of service. The CDU does not refer content from journalists or politicians to platforms, a policy designed to preserve freedom of political debate.
Complementing the CDU's operational work is the RESIST 2 Counter-disinformation toolkit, a systematic framework developed for government communicators. This toolkit provides an evidence-based methodology for identifying, monitoring, and responding to disinformation, with a strong emphasis on building societal and individual resilience through proactive communication and public awareness campaigns.
The most significant and controversial element of the UK's strategy is legislative. The Online Safety Bill is an ambitious piece of legislation that aims to create a new regulatory framework for the digital space. It would establish requirements for search engines and user-to-user platforms to address illegal material, but more contentiously, it would also compel the largest platforms to create and enforce terms of service to address certain categories of "legal but harmful" content, which is likely to include state-sponsored disinformation. While this would put the onus of moderation on the platforms rather than a government enforcer, the bill has faced intense criticism from civil liberties groups who argue that it could lead to the over-removal of legitimate speech and create a chilling effect on free expression. The UK's experience thus highlights the same fundamental challenge facing all Western democracies: how to legislate against harmful information without damaging the democratic principles they seek to protect.
The European Union and NATO: A Coordinated, Multi-Layered Defense
The European Union and NATO have developed the most coordinated and multi-layered defense against Russian disinformation, leveraging their multilateral structures to create a broad-based response.
The EU's strategy is distinctly regulatory and political. It has deployed its sanctions powers to directly ban the broadcasting of major Russian state media outlets. Its landmark Digital Services Act (DSA) imposes significant new obligations on very large online platforms, requiring them to conduct systemic risk assessments of how their services can be used to spread disinformation and to take measures to mitigate those risks. This shifts the focus from moderating individual pieces of content to addressing the underlying architectural features of platforms that allow disinformation to proliferate. Politically, the EU's East Stratcom Task Force and its public-facing EUvsDisinfo platform actively monitor and debunk pro-Kremlin narratives, providing a constant stream of factual counter-messaging. These efforts are backed by strong political will from the European Parliament, which has passed numerous resolutions calling for a robust and unified European response to Russian information warfare.
NATO approaches disinformation as a core component of modern hybrid warfare, integrating its response into its overall defense and deterrence posture. The Alliance's strategy is based on four key functions: understanding the information environment, preventing the effectiveness of threats, containing specific incidents, and recovering stronger by learning lessons. Key operational elements include the deployment of Counter-Hybrid Support Teams that can provide tailored assistance to any Ally upon request. NATO's Strategic Communications units actively counter propaganda with factual information, aiming not to win a propaganda war but to provide credible alternatives.
Crucially, NATO's strategy emphasizes cooperation and partnerships. It works closely with the EU on countering hybrid threats and has established dedicated platforms for cooperation with partners, most notably the NATO-Ukraine Platform on Countering Hybrid Warfare, created in 2016 to help Ukraine build its own resilience. The Alliance also leverages a network of affiliated Centres of Excellence, including the Strategic Communications COE in Riga, Latvia, and the Cooperative Cyber Defence COE in Tallinn, Estonia, which serve as hubs for research, training, and the development of expertise. This collaborative, multi-layered approach allows NATO and the EU to address the threat on multiple fronts simultaneously, from high-level policy and regulation down to tactical training and public communication. This highlights a divergence in strategic philosophy across the West: the European approach leans more heavily towards "content regulation" through measures like the DSA and broadcasting bans, while the U.S. approach, constrained by its legal and political culture, is pivoting more toward "resilience building" through education and public awareness.
Synthesis and Strategic Recommendations for Democratic Resilience
The preceding analysis reveals a modern conflict waged across multiple, interconnected domains. Russia's military aggression in Ukraine is inextricably linked to its information war against the West and its attempts to subvert the international legal order. Ukraine's innovative use of blockchain technology, the West's deployment of sanctions, and the evolving jurisprudence of international courts are all facets of a democratic world grappling with how to defend itself against a hybrid adversary. To enhance this defense, Western democracies must adopt a more integrated, proactive, and resilient strategic posture.
Integrated Threat Assessment: The Symbiosis of Kinetic, Economic, and Information Warfare
It is a strategic error to view Russia's actions as a series of separate crises. They are components of a single, integrated hybrid warfare strategy. The Kremlin's information warfare is not an adjunct to its military operations; it is a preparatory and enabling element. The false narrative of a "genocide" in the Donbas was not mere rhetoric; it was the manufactured casus belli for a kinetic invasion, a pretext that was subsequently dismantled by the International Court of Justice. The purpose of the "firehose of falsehood" is to create a permissive environment for military aggression by eroding Western political will, fracturing alliances, and confusing public opinion.
The Western response, in turn, demonstrates this interconnectedness. Economic sanctions are not only aimed at Russia's defense industry but also at its propaganda machine, seeking to degrade the capabilities of outlets like RT and Sputnik. International legal forums, once seen as venues for resolving peacetime disputes, have become another battlefield where Ukraine challenges Russia's narratives and Russia uses the proceedings themselves as fodder for new disinformation campaigns. Ukraine's use of blockchain to both fund its military and create an immutable record of war crimes is a direct, technologically-enabled response that operates in both the economic and information domains simultaneously. Acknowledging this symbiotic relationship is the first step toward crafting a truly comprehensive counter-strategy.
Policy Recommendations for a Whole-of-Society Defense
To effectively counter Russia's integrated hybrid warfare, Western democracies must move beyond siloed responses and adopt a coordinated, whole-of-society defense. The following recommendations are designed to strengthen collective democratic resilience against information warfare.
1. Harmonize and Strengthen Sanctions Enforcement: The effectiveness of sanctions against disinformation actors is currently undermined by inconsistent enforcement and easily exploited loopholes. A dedicated Transatlantic Task Force on Countering Malign Information Finance and Operations should be established. This body, comprising representatives from the U.S. Treasury and State Departments, the European Commission, and UK authorities, would be mandated to harmonize sanctions lists, share intelligence on circumvention techniques (such as mirror sites and the use of shell companies), and coordinate enforcement actions against platforms and service providers that enable sanctioned entities.
2. Invest in Proactive "Information Inoculation": The current model of reactive debunking is insufficient, as falsehoods often spread faster than fact-checks. Western governments should shift resources toward a proactive "pre-bunking" or "inoculation" model. This involves using intelligence analysis to anticipate likely Russian disinformation narratives and proactively exposing the tactics and themes to the public before the campaigns are launched. This approach, which aligns with the principles of the UK's RESIST 2 toolkit and CISA's "rumor control" initiative, builds psychological resilience by equipping citizens to recognize and dismiss manipulative content when they encounter it.
3. Develop a Robust International Legal Framework for Information Warfare: The existing legal framework is being tested and adapted, but gaps remain. Allied nations should champion the development of a new international treaty or an addendum to existing instruments like the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. This framework should, building on the ICJ's ruling in Ukraine v. Russia and the legal analysis of the Romanian election case, explicitly define and prohibit hostile, state-sponsored information operations aimed at subverting democratic processes or justifying illegal aggression. It should establish clear international norms, attribution standards, and a menu of permissible countermeasures for victim states.
4. Foster Resilient Public-Private Partnerships for Information Integrity: The relationship between governments and technology platforms is often adversarial. A more effective model would be the creation of formal, trusted, and transparent partnerships. The creation of a Social Media Data and Threat Analysis Center, as proposed by the U.S. Cyberspace Solarium Commission, is a promising model. Such a center, operating with a clear public mandate and strict privacy protections, would allow vetted academic and non-profit researchers access to platform data to analyze foreign influence campaigns, while providing platforms with timely, actionable intelligence from government sources. This would depoliticize collaboration and build a shared understanding of the threat.
5. Embrace Asymmetric Transparency: Democracies should weaponize their own core value of transparency against adversaries who thrive in the shadows. Learning from Ukraine's use of blockchain to create verifiable records of war crimes , allied nations should invest in and promote the use of technologies that create immutable, publicly auditable records in areas frequently targeted by disinformation. This could include blockchain-based systems for election result reporting, tracking of foreign aid distribution, and verification of supply chains for critical goods, thereby reducing the attack surface for malign influence.
6. Build a Coalition for Democratic Information Resilience: The shared threat of information warfare necessitates a more formal allied response. The U.S., EU, UK, Canada, Australia, and other key partners should establish a Coalition for Democratic Information Resilience. Modeled on security alliances, this coalition would not be a military pact, but a political and technical one dedicated to sharing intelligence on information threats, coordinating counter-messaging and sanctions, developing joint standards for platform accountability, and pooling resources to support independent media and civil society in vulnerable nations. This would institutionalize the ad-hoc cooperation that currently exists and signal a united front against authoritarian information manipulation.
